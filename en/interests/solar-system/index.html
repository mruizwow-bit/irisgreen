The planets

The eight planets in order of distance from the Sun, and the Sun. Each entry puts the main points first and then the data.

Star

Sun

The Sun is a star: a ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. It holds 99.86% of all the mass in the Solar System.

What it looks like

Its visible surface, the photosphere, is at about 5,500 °C. It has darker, slightly cooler spots that come and go.

What stands out

  • Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach the Earth.
  • About 1.3 million Earths would fit inside the Sun.
  • It spins faster at the equator, in about 25 days, than near the poles, in about 34 days.
  • It is about 4,600 million years old. It is roughly halfway through its life.

How to see it

Never look straight at the Sun, with your eyes, binoculars or a telescope: it damages your sight in seconds. To observe it you need solar glasses or filters that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard, or you can project its image onto a sheet of paper.

Rocky planet

Mercury

The smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It is made of rock and metal and has almost no atmosphere.

What it looks like

Grey and covered in craters, like our Moon.

What stands out

  • A year on Mercury lasts 88 Earth days.
  • A full day, from one sunrise to the next, lasts 176 Earth days: longer than its year.
  • It goes from about 430 °C in the day to about −180 °C at night, because there is no air to keep the heat in.
  • Its iron core takes up almost 85% of its radius.

How to see it

You can see it with the naked eye, but always close to the Sun: shortly after sunset, low in the west, or shortly before sunrise, low in the east. Do not look for it with binoculars while the Sun is above the horizon.

Rocky planet

Venus

Rocky and almost the same size as the Earth, but with a very thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide and clouds of sulphuric acid.

What it looks like

From space it looks like a smooth, yellowish ball: we only see the clouds. The ground is hidden underneath.

What stands out

  • It is the hottest planet: about 464 °C on the ground, hotter than Mercury, because of the greenhouse effect.
  • It spins the other way from almost every planet, and very slowly: one turn takes 243 Earth days.
  • The pressure on the ground is 92 times the Earth’s: like being about 900 metres under the sea.
  • We know its ground thanks to radar, which goes through the clouds.

How to see it

It is the brightest thing in the sky after the Sun and the Moon. You see it at dusk in the west or at dawn in the east. That is why it is called the morning or evening star.

Rocky planet

Earth

Our planet. It is the only place where we know there is life and liquid water on the surface.

What it looks like

Blue from the oceans, which cover 71% of the surface, with white clouds and green, brown and white continents. At night you can see city lights.

What stands out

  • Its axis is tilted by 23.4°: that is why there are seasons.
  • It is about 150 million km from the Sun. That distance is used as a unit: the astronomical unit (au).
  • Its magnetic field protects us from the solar wind and makes the auroras.
  • It is the densest planet in the Solar System.

How to see it

We see it from the inside. At dusk or dawn you can watch the International Space Station go by. It flies about 400 km up and goes round the Earth every hour and a half.

Rocky planet

Mars

The red planet. It is rocky, a little over half as wide as the Earth, and has a very thin atmosphere.

What it looks like

Reddish because of the iron oxide in its dust. It has white ice caps at the poles.

What stands out

  • It has the largest known volcano, Olympus Mons: about 22 km high, almost three times Everest.
  • A day on Mars lasts 24 hours and 40 minutes, almost like ours.
  • It has a canyon, Valles Marineris, about 4,000 km long.
  • It has two small, potato-shaped moons: Phobos and Deimos.

How to see it

You can see it with the naked eye as an orange dot that does not twinkle. About every 26 months it is at opposition: then it is closer, brighter and visible all night.

Gas giant

Jupiter

The largest planet. It is a giant made of gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, and has no solid ground.

What it looks like

Bands of cream, brown and white clouds, and the Great Red Spot: a storm wider than the Earth.

What stands out

  • It has 2.5 times more mass than all the other planets put together.
  • It spins very fast: a day lasts less than 10 hours.
  • Galileo discovered its four large moons in 1610: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.
  • It has very thin, dark rings, discovered by Voyager 1 in 1979.

How to see it

Very bright, with a steady white light. With binoculars held still you can see its four large moons as tiny dots in a line.

Gas giant

Saturn

A gas giant famous for its rings. It is the second largest planet.

What it looks like

Pale yellow with soft bands. Its rings are made of pieces of ice and some rock, from dust grains to blocks the size of a house.

What stands out

  • Its density is so low, 0.69 g/cm³, that overall it is less dense than water.
  • The main rings are about 270,000 km from side to side, but mostly only about 10 metres thick.
  • Titan, its largest moon, has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane.
  • It is the planet with the most known moons.

How to see it

You can see it with the naked eye as a yellowish star that does not twinkle. A small telescope already shows the rings.

Ice giant

Uranus

An ice giant: inside it has water, ammonia and methane, under an atmosphere of hydrogen and helium.

What it looks like

Blue-green and almost featureless. The methane in its atmosphere absorbs red light.

What stands out

  • It spins on its side: its axis is tilted by 98°. Each pole has 42 years of day and 42 years of night.
  • It was the first planet found with a telescope: William Herschel discovered it in 1781.
  • It has 13 thin, dark rings.
  • Its moons are named after characters by Shakespeare and Alexander Pope.

How to see it

Under a very dark sky it is right at the limit of what the naked eye can see. With binoculars it is easy if you know where to look.

Ice giant

Neptune

The planet farthest from the Sun. It is an ice giant, like Uranus.

What it looks like

Deep blue, with white clouds and dark spots that come and go.

What stands out

  • It has the fastest winds measured on any planet: over 2,000 km/h.
  • It was discovered in 1846 thanks to mathematics: Urbain Le Verrier worked out where it had to be and Johann Galle found it there.
  • It takes about 164 years to go once round the Sun.
  • Its moon Triton orbits the opposite way to the planet’s spin.

How to see it

You cannot see it with the naked eye. A small telescope shows it as a tiny bluish dot.

Dwarf planets

A dwarf planet goes round the Sun and is almost round because of its own gravity, but it has not cleared its orbit of other objects. The International Astronomical Union recognises five.

Dwarf planet

Ceres

The dwarf planet closest to the Sun. It is the largest object in the asteroid belt, between Mars and Jupiter.

What it looks like

Dark grey, with craters and a few very bright patches of salts, like those in Occator crater.

What stands out

  • Giuseppe Piazzi discovered it on 1 January 1801. For half a century it was counted as a planet.
  • It is about 940 km across: about the straight-line distance from London to Berlin, some 930 km.
  • Under its surface there may still be salty water from an old ocean.

How to see it

With binoculars it looks like a faint star. You need a sky chart to know which one it is.

Dwarf planet

Pluto

A dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt, beyond Neptune. It was counted as the ninth planet until 2006.

What it looks like

Cream, brown and reddish tones. It has a large heart-shaped plain of ice, Sputnik Planitia.

What stands out

  • In 2006 the International Astronomical Union moved it into the dwarf planet category.
  • Its moon Charon is so big that the two go round a point between them, outside Pluto.
  • It has mountains of water ice more than 3 km high.
  • Clyde Tombaugh discovered it in 1930.

How to see it

Only with a large telescope: it shines like a magnitude 14 star.

Dwarf planet

Haumea

A dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt. It is very elongated, shaped like a rugby ball.

What it looks like

There are no close-up images. From the light it reflects we know its surface has water ice.

What stands out

  • It spins once every 3.9 hours. That is why it is so stretched.
  • It has a ring, discovered in 2017.
  • It has two moons: Hiʻiaka and Namaka.
  • The IAU recorded the Sierra Nevada Observatory (Granada, Spain) as the place of its discovery, in 2005. The name was proposed by Mike Brown’s team, who had also detected it.

How to see it

Only with large telescopes: magnitude 17.

Dwarf planet

Makemake

A dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt, a little smaller than Pluto.

What it looks like

There are no close-up images. From the light it reflects we know it is reddish and has methane ice.

What stands out

  • Mike Brown’s team discovered it in 2005, a few days after Easter. It is named after the creator god of the Rapa Nui people of Easter Island.
  • It has a small, dark moon, discovered in 2016.
  • It is so far away that sunlight reaches it about 2,000 times weaker than at the Earth.

How to see it

Only with large telescopes: magnitude 17.

Dwarf planet

Eris

The most massive dwarf planet known, and the farthest of the five that are recognised.

What it looks like

There are no close-up images. It is very white: it reflects almost all light, perhaps because of methane ice.

What stands out

  • Its discovery in 2005 led the IAU to define in 2006 what a planet is.
  • It has 26% more mass than Pluto, although it is slightly smaller.
  • It takes about 559 years to go once round the Sun.
  • It has one moon, Dysnomia.

How to see it

Only with large telescopes: almost magnitude 19.

How the positions are worked out

  • Planets and Pluto: JPL approximate Keplerian elements, valid from 1800 to 2050. Compared with a high-precision calculation (astronomy-engine), the error is under 10 arcminutes, a third of the size of the full Moon: you cannot notice it with the naked eye.
  • Ceres, Haumea, Makemake and Eris: Minor Planet Center elements from 2025, carried forward with their mean motion.
  • The Moon: low-precision formulas, with an error of a few tenths of a degree between 1950 and 2050. Outside those years the error grows.
  • The other moons are placed at their real distance and with their real period, but not in their exact position for today.
  • Orientation and spin of each body: poles and prime meridians of the International Astronomical Union (WGCCRE 2015).
