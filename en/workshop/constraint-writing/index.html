The workshop

Constraint writing

A tricky rule sparks ideas. Choose one or several, write, and see straight away what you meet and what you do not. Every warning has a “Go” button that takes you to that part of the text.

What you can make here

  • A micro-story of exactly 50 words
  • A text without the letter e (a lipogram)
  • An acrostic with the name of your favourite animal
  • A 5-7-5 haiku or a 14-line sonnet
  • A story built on the story spine or another fixed structure
  • Your text as .txt, .md, accessible HTML or PDF

How it works

  1. Write in the editor in the centre. Above it you can see whether your text meets the rules.
  2. In Properties, switch on the rules you want: you can combine them.
  3. Press “Go” next to a warning, or “Next warning” (F8 key), to jump to that part of the text.
  4. Structure lists your drafts and the parts of the text: lines or paragraphs. Choose a part to see its syllables or its words.
  5. Save the project or export your text from File.

This studio is an interactive tool and needs JavaScript. Below you will find the full explanation of how it works and what you learn.

Writing with rules

A constraint is a rule you set yourself when you write. The Oulipo group (France, 1960) turned it into a literary game: Georges Perec wrote a whole novel without the letter e.

Here you can combine the lipogram (a missing letter), word count, character limit, number of paragraphs, acrostic, tautogram, univocalism, palindrome, haiku, sonnet and fixed story structure.

Syllables in English

For drafts in English, the syllable counter is approximate: it counts groups of vowels and leaves out a silent final e and the -es and -ed endings that are not pronounced. English sonnets are checked as 14 lines of 10 syllables. Trust your ear when it disagrees.

Spanish metre

For drafts in Spanish, syllables are split with the rules in the Spanish Royal Academy’s Orthography (diphthongs, hiatus, clusters such as pr or bl). Synalepha joins a final vowel to the first vowel of the next word, and the count changes by one when the line ends on a stressed final syllable (+1) or three syllables from the end (−1). Both can be switched off in Properties.

Story structures

The story spine comes from Kenn Adams: “Once upon a time…”, “Every day…”, “But one day…”, “Because of that…”, “Because of that…”, “Until finally…”, “And ever since then…”. Each opening phrase starts a paragraph. There are also templates made for the workshop: three paragraphs, a letter to your past self and the diary of an object.

Plain language

The statistics help you write text that is easy to read: words, sentences, average sentence length, long words and reading time (about 200 words a minute). Sentences of more than 25 words get their own “Go” button. They are guidance, not marks: a poem can break them on purpose.

Your work and your files

What you make stays in this browser tab. It is not sent anywhere and the workshop does not store it in the browser.

To keep it, open File and press “Save project”: a file downloads to your device. To carry on another day, open File, press “Open…” and choose that file. Exports (images, sound, 3D models, games) are also files that you download yourself.
