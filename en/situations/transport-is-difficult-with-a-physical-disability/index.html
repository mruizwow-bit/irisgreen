“Transport or public spaces are difficult because I also have a physical disability”

Daily life

This page describes an everyday situation, not a diagnosis. The same situation can have several explanations, and a professional assessment is what distinguishes between them.

In brief

The station lift has been broken for weeks, the ramp leads to a step, or the designated seat is occupied. This is not simply about the journey being tiring; sometimes the journey is physically inaccessible.

It does not automatically mean

  • An everyday activity being difficult does not equal lack of autonomy and does not point to a diagnosis on its own.

What to look at

  • The exact barrier: a step, distance, seating, a lift, or the gap between platform and vehicle.

What may help now

  • Check accessibility conditions for the route in advance and, where possible, check their current status on the day.

What to avoid

  • Planning as though lifts, ramps or assistance are infallible; have an alternative when possible.

When to seek professional help

Ask the transport operator, accessibility services or rehabilitation/occupational therapy professionals for guidance if assistive devices or travel adaptations are needed. If new pain, loss of function or new physical fatigue appears, also seek healthcare assessment.

Warning signs

A transport barrier is not a medical emergency in itself. Chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, sudden weakness or new loss of function need urgent medical attention.

Sources and review

  • F01 · WHO · ICF / International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health
