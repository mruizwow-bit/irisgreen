How they are discovered

Almost no exoplanet can be seen directly: they are too far away and their star outshines them. They are discovered by what they do to their star’s light.

Transit

4,708 planets

The planet passes in front of its star and blocks a little of its light. The star looks slightly fainter for a few hours, every time the planet goes round.

The dip in light gives the planet’s size; the time between dips gives how long it takes to go round.

The first one: OGLE-TR-56 b (2002)

Radial velocity

1,200 planets

The planet pulls on its star and makes it move a little, towards us and away from us. That wobble slightly changes the colour of its light.

The size of the wobble gives the planet’s minimum mass.

The first one: 51 Peg b (1995)

Gravitational microlensing

289 planets

A star passes in front of a more distant one and its gravity acts as a magnifying glass: the background star looks brighter for days. If the front star has a planet, an extra, very short peak appears.

Each event happens only once: the observation cannot be repeated.

The first one: OGLE-2003-BLG-235L b (2004)

Direct imaging

97 planets

The planet is photographed. The star’s light, millions of times brighter, has to be blocked.

It works for large, young, hot planets far from their star.

The first one: 2MASS J12073346-3932539 b (2004)

Pulsar timing

8 planets

A pulsar is what is left of a star that exploded. It sends very regular radio pulses; if they arrive early and late in a rhythm, there are planets around it.

This is how the first exoplanets were found, in 1992.

The first one: PSR B1257+12 c (1992)

Transit timing variations

29 planets

A planet that already passes in front of its star arrives a few minutes early or late. That gives away another planet pulling on it.

The first one: Kepler-19 c (2011)

Eclipse timing variations

17 planets

In a pair of stars that eclipse each other, the eclipses come early or late in a regular pattern because of a planet going round both.

The first one: DP Leo b (2009)

Astrometry

6 planets

The star’s position in the sky is measured very precisely. The planet makes it trace a small circle.

The first one: DENIS-P J082303.1-491201 b (2013)

Orbital brightness modulation

9 planets

The total brightness of the star and planet changes along the orbit: the planet reflects light and slightly distorts the star.

The first one: KOI-55 b (2011)

Pulsation timing variations

2 planets

Some stars pulse with a steady rhythm. A planet makes that rhythm arrive earlier or later.

The first one: V0391 Peg b (2007)

Disk kinematics

1 planets

In the disc of gas and dust where planets are born, a young planet leaves a mark on how the gas moves.

The first one: HD 97048 b (2019)

Proxima Cen

Proxima Centauri

The star closest to the Sun, 4.2 light years away. It has an Earth-sized planet that gets about two thirds of the light we get from the Sun.

Proxima is a small, cool red dwarf that sends out strong flares. It is not known whether the planet has an atmosphere.

51 Peg

51 Pegasi

The first planet found round a star like the Sun, in 1995. Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 2019.

It is a “hot Jupiter”: a gas giant that goes round its star in only 4.2 days. It is named Dimidium; its star, Helvetios.

They are named Draugr, Poltergeist and Phobetor; the pulsar is named Lich.

How the map is made

  • Each star’s position on the map comes from its right ascension, declination and distance in NASA’s table. In the “compressed” view distance is drawn on a logarithmic scale so that near and far stars show at the same time.
  • Colour of each star: an approximation of a black body’s colour at its temperature. Temperate zone: between 0.95 and 1.67 times the square root of the star’s luminosity, in astronomical units; it is only a guide.
  • The Milky Way in the background is the same one as in Night sky.
