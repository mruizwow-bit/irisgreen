Situations

Everyday situation entries. Each one says what to look at, what may help and when to ask for an assessment.

This page describes an everyday situation, not a diagnosis. The same situation can have several explanations, and a professional assessment is what distinguishes between them.

Sleep“I go to bed tired and sleep doesn't come”You go to bed tired and still take an hour, or two, to fall asleep. The harder you try, the more awake your mind can feel. Communication“I take a long time to answer and someone answers for me”The question arrives, the answer exists, and it takes time. Communication“I repeat words and phrases”Repeating words or phrases can serve different functions: communicating, processing language, sustaining an interaction, remembering or regulating. Education and work“I need an adjustment in class”Something in the classroom is getting in the way of showing what you know: noise, time pressure, the exam format or copying from the board. An adjustment does not change what is being assessed; it changes the route by which you can show it. Education and work“I need adjustments at work”The work gets done, but at a cost: an open-plan office, back-to-back meetings, the phone, or changes with no warning. Asking for an adjustment is not asking to do less work; it is asking to remove a barrier to doing it. Education and work“I need an adjustment in class or at work, but I don't know how to ask for it”You know what is getting in the way but not who to tell, what words to use or whether asking will cause problems. While the process is unclear, the request may never get made and the same barrier remains. Education and work“I want to do it, but I can't get started”The task is decided and time has been set aside, yet the time disappears while you look at it, get up for water and look at it again. Sometimes what is missing is one concrete first action: what to touch first, where to do it and what needs to be ready. Education and work“I can only get started when something interests me a lot”With something interesting, three hours can pass without looking up; with the task beside it, even if it is just as short, you may not get past the first line. Interest can function as a strong trigger for engagement. Education and work“A large task blocks me before I even start”The task fits into one sentence — ‘move house’, ‘submit the assignment’ — and the block arrives before anything has been touched. Inside that one sentence are many steps that nobody has written down, and there is no obvious entry point. Education and work“I put off even things I genuinely want to do”Saturday's plan is something you wanted, Saturday arrives, and it gets postponed again. Desired activities can be put off too, especially when the moment of starting has something uncomfortable attached to it: a phone call, a wait, a doubt or an unfamiliar place. Education and work“A small change disrupts my whole day”The time or place of an appointment changes, and what falls apart is not only that hour but the sequence that had already been built around it. From the outside the change looks small; rebuilding the plan may not be. Education and work“Switching from one task to another blocks me”The task that was in progress does not let go, and the next one does not start. The time in between disappears, and from the outside it can look like wasted time when what is actually difficult is the transition itself. Daily life“I need help with a medical appointment”The appointment is on Thursday and it has been taking up mental space since Monday: what they will ask, how long the wait will be, who will speak. Many practical supports can be requested in advance, but people are not always told that they can ask. Daily life“I can't explain my symptoms to the doctor”In the waiting room it was clear what you wanted to say, but inside the consultation it comes out in the wrong order, or the minor details come out and the important part stays unsaid. You are being asked to remember, organise, summarise and decide at the same time, often within a short appointment. Daily life“The hospital overwhelms me”Before you have spoken to anyone there may already be echo, bright lights, announcements and an open-ended wait. The load comes from the accumulation, and by the time your turn arrives there may be little capacity left for explaining. Eating“Eating away from home is very difficult”The difficulty may be the food itself: the texture is different from usual, the smell arrives before the plate, and nothing on the menu feels manageable without negotiation. The person may leave hungry and self-conscious. Eating“Eating in a restaurant is too much for me”The food itself may be fine and the venue may not be: loud music, closely packed tables, staff asking questions while you are eating, or feeling watched. The place, rather than the plate, can be what uses the energy. Eating“Eating out is too unpredictable”What weighs most is what is not yet known: how the dish will arrive, how long things will take, whether what is described on the menu will match what is served. You may eat adequately and still have spent much of the day anticipating the uncertainty. Sensory experiences“They don't notice hunger or thirst”The morning passes without drinking or asking for food, not necessarily because the person does not want either, but because the signal may not be noticed or communicated. Headache, irritability or an energy drop may appear first. Sensory experiences“I don't know whether I'm tired, overloaded or hungry”There is a sense that something is wrong but it is hard to tell which need is involved. You try eating and that was not it; you try lying down and that was not it either. Time can disappear into testing possibilities while the discomfort grows. Sensory experiences“Hunger hits suddenly and by then I feel awful”There may be no noticeable hunger all morning, and then shaking, dizziness, irritability and an urgent need to eat arrive together. The important point is not to assume a diagnosis from those symptoms, but to notice that the signal may have arrived late. Relationships“I'm exploring my gender”Trying a name, asking to be addressed differently, changing something about clothing and noticing how it feels. None of these steps commits a person to anything later, and being able to explore somewhere without having to justify it can make a major difference. Relationships“I'm exploring my gender and I don't have a word for it yet”You may know that something does not fit while still not having a word that describes it. People around you may ask for a label immediately — ‘so what are you?’ — as though respectful treatment depends on having one. Relationships“I'm autistic and I'm also trans or non-binary”One service says autism has to be dealt with first; another says gender has to be resolved first. The person is sent back and forth with the same question unanswered and time lost in the process. Relationships“The way I dress doesn't match what people expect of my gender”The clothes that do not squeeze, itch or restrict movement may also be the clothes other people do not expect. In some settings that brings comments or even risk, although the original decision may simply have been about getting through the day comfortably. Relationships“My experience of gender changes over time”What fit last year may not fit in the same way today. That does not erase the earlier experience or make it a mistake. The difficulty is often external: paperwork and other people may expect one fixed answer. Relationships“People keep using a name I no longer use”You have already said it several times and the old name still appears: on the class list, in work email or when you are called in a waiting room. Sometimes it is not only a person forgetting; the old name is still stored somewhere in a system. Relationships“I don't want my previous name to appear in front of other people”The name may be corrected on a current list and still appear in an old email, on a certificate or on a waiting-room screen. At that point this is not simply a data correction: private information may be being disclosed to people who do not need it. Relationships“I don't know which pronoun to use for someone”Someone is about to join a meeting or arrive in class, and you do not know how to refer to them without getting it wrong. Asking respectfully can take only a few seconds, and a mistake can usually be corrected briefly and then left behind. Relationships“Using another name makes me feel more comfortable”With the new name, the day may take less effort: you respond more readily and enter situations differently. That experience is enough to matter; it does not need a further explanation to be valid. Daily life“I feel I have to look normal to fit in”You watch how other people speak, how much eye contact they use, when they laugh, and you copy it. It may work socially, while also adding a layer of effort that other people in the room may not be doing. Daily life“I feel as though I'm acting all day to look normal”It is not a short performance: it can be a whole day of monitoring tone, facial expression, movement and the urge to leave. From the outside everything may appear to be going well; the cost may become visible only at home, when little energy remains. Daily life“I feel I have to hide parts of myself to fit in”This is not about performing in general but hiding specific things: an interest you could talk about for hours, a movement that helps you regulate, or the need to leave earlier. People often hide these things because showing them went badly somewhere before. Daily life“At work I use all my energy trying to look as though I'm fine”The work gets done, and it gets done well. What is left out is everything else: shopping, a phone call, dinner, seeing someone. The working day is paid for in the hours afterwards, and that cost may not show up in a performance review. Relationships“I don't know what information is private”You share something normally and realise from the other person's reaction that it may not have been information for that setting. Privacy is not always intuitive; it can help to treat it as a classification that can be learned explicitly. Relationships“I share information and afterwards think it was too private”You leave the conversation and the replay begins: I shouldn't have said that. Sometimes there is a real privacy issue and sometimes the continuing problem is rumination; from the inside, the two can feel very similar. Sensory experiences“Noise is unbearable for them”In the playground, supermarket or a family meal, the person covers their ears, leaves or becomes upset, and from the outside it can look as though they went from zero to one hundred. The environment may have been building up sound for a long time beforehand. Sensory experiences“Exams in a noisy classroom are unbearable”Coughs, chairs, a clock, someone tapping a pen: in an ordinary lesson these sounds may be manageable, but during an exam they compete with the content that must be held in mind. The student may know the material and still struggle to demonstrate it in that setting. Sensory experiences“Ordinary sounds are painful for me”A plate in the sink, a motorbike outside, applause: it does not merely feel annoying, it hurts, and the pain can continue for a while after the sound stops. Other people may consider the volume ordinary. Sensory experiences“Specific sounds trigger an immediate reaction in me”Chewing, a clicking pen or breathing can trigger a sudden rush of anger or distress, bodily tension and an urge to leave. The key issue may not be loudness: even a quiet version of the same sound can trigger the reaction. Daily life“Public transport overwhelms me”Twenty minutes standing, someone talking on the phone beside you, sudden braking, an announcement you cannot make out. You can arrive having already spent much of the energy needed for what comes next. Daily life“Transport or public spaces are difficult because I also have a physical disability”The station lift has been broken for weeks, the ramp leads to a step, or the designated seat is occupied. This is not simply about the journey being tiring; sometimes the journey is physically inaccessible.
