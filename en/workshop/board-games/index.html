The workshop

Board games

Make a game and play it the same day. Draw the board, design the cards, write the rules, try a game and use numbers to check whether it is fair.

What you can make here

  • A dice race with special squares
  • A balanced deck of cards
  • A strategy game on hexagons
  • The rules as HTML or Markdown
  • The whole game ready to print on A4 sheets

How it works

  1. Choose the kind of board (track, grid or hexagons) and paint the squares: start, finish, go forward, go back, miss a turn or draw a card.
  2. Add cards in Structure and give them a title, text, value, icon and colour. Copies are printed repeatedly.
  3. Write the rules with the template: objective, setup, turn, ending and variants. The studio counts the rules and checks them for plain language.
  4. Press “Play” to try a game: roll the dice, move the pieces, draw cards and pass the turn.
  5. Open “Balance” to see the probability of each dice total and to simulate thousands of games. Then print the game from File.

Three kinds of board

The track is a row of numbered squares in a zigzag, from start to finish: your piece moves as far as the dice say. The grid and the hexagons are maps you move across square by square, and hexagons have six neighbours instead of four.

Each kind of square has its own colour and drawing, so you can recognise it even if you cannot see the colours well. With the keyboard, the arrow keys move the cursor, Enter paints and the square brackets change the kind of square.

Cards to print

Cards are 63 × 88 mm, the size most board games use. The print sheet puts nine cards on an A4 with cut marks at the corners. When printing, choose “actual size” or “100%” so they come out the right size.

Playing and balancing

In play mode randomness only happens when you press “Roll dice”, and the result is announced in words. Pieces move with the keyboard or by tapping a square.

In Balance you see the exact probability of each dice total and a Monte Carlo simulation: the studio plays thousands of quick games and counts how many turns they take and how often each player wins. That shows whether the first player has an advantage and what the special squares change. The exact probability comes from combining the faces of each die (Grinstead and Snell, 1997).

Clear rules

The template separates objective, setup, turn, ending and variants, the usual order in game rulebooks (Selinker, 2011). The check warns about sentences longer than twenty words and steps with no verb, following the plain-language idea: short sentences that say who does what. The counter tells you how many rules you have, for the five-rule challenge.

Sources

Selinker, Mike (ed.) (2011). The Kobold Guide to Board Game Design. Kirkland (Washington): Open Design.

Grinstead, Charles M. and Snell, J. Laurie (1997). Introduction to Probability (2nd revised edition). Providence (Rhode Island): American Mathematical Society.

Your work and your files

What you make stays in this browser tab. It is not sent anywhere and the workshop does not store it in the browser.

To keep it, open File and press “Save project”: a file downloads to your device. To carry on another day, open File, press “Open…” and choose that file. Exports (images, sound, 3D models, games) are also files that you download yourself.
